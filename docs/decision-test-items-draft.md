# Decision test: item bank, draft

**Status: for the owner's review, item by item.** All thirty, plus three
practice items. Batch 1 was fourteen, A1 to D2; batch 2 adds the
remaining sixteen and the practice set.

Every item is accept, reject, or rewrite. **A dead lure is a wasted
question out of only thirty**, so rejecting is cheap here and expensive
later.

## The bar each item has to clear

1. **Readable and answerable in about 20 seconds**, including rating
   confidence. Two or three lines.
2. **A wrong answer that arrives fast and feels right.** If the lure
   does not pull, the item measures nothing under load.
3. **A correct answer that needs deliberation**, not knowledge. Nobody
   should fail for not knowing a fact.
4. **Not a circulated classic.** Bat-and-ball, widgets, lily pads and
   Linda are excluded: they would measure prior exposure. Some items
   below share a *structure* with a classic, which is unavoidable and
   fine, but none shares its surface.
5. **Generic**, per the FSD. No plant or industry framing.

Multiple choice, three or four options, the lure always present. Choice
rather than free entry because it is faster to answer inside the budget
and because it makes correct, lure and other unambiguous to score.

All arithmetic below is machine-verified.

## Family A: arithmetic traps

The fast answer comes from a plausible operation that is the wrong one.

**A1.** A notebook and a pencil cost $5.40 together. The notebook costs
$5.00 more than the pencil. How much is the pencil?
`$0.20` · **lure `$0.40`** · `$0.45` · `$1.08`
*Subtracting instead of solving for two unknowns.*

**A2.** Six machines take 6 minutes to make 6 parts. How long would 100
machines take to make 100 parts?
`6 minutes` · **lure `100 minutes`** · `60 minutes` · `1 minute`
*Matching the numbers instead of finding the rate.*

**A3.** Algae on a pond doubles every day and covers the whole pond on
day 30. On which day was the pond half covered?
`Day 29` · **lure `Day 15`** · `Day 28` · `Day 16`
*Halving the endpoint instead of stepping back one doubling.*

**A4.** A jacket costs $80. It is cut by 25% in a sale, then the sale
price is raised by 25% the following week. What does it cost now?
`$75` · **lure `$80`** · `$85` · `$70`
*Assuming equal percentages cancel, when they apply to different bases.*

**A5.** You drive 60 miles at 30 mph, then the same 60 miles back at 60
mph. What is your average speed for the trip?
`40 mph` · **lure `45 mph`** · `50 mph` · `30 mph`
*Averaging the speeds rather than dividing distance by total time.*

**A6.** A shirt is marked down 40%, then a further 20% comes off at the
till. What is the total discount?
`52%` · **lure `60%`** · `48%` · `56%`
*Adding the percentages.*

**A7.** A train covers 90 miles in 90 minutes. How far does it travel in
one hour?
`60 miles` · **lure `90 miles`** · `75 miles` · `45 miles`
*The matching numbers invite the answer before the units are read.*

**A8.** If 5 painters take 5 hours to paint 5 rooms, how long does 1
painter take to paint 1 room?
`5 hours` · **lure `1 hour`** · `25 hours` · `½ hour`
*Dividing through, when the per-painter rate is unchanged.*

## Family B: base rates and ratios

The diagnostic detail is vivid and the base rate is not.

**B1.** One person in 100 has a condition. A test catches 90% of those
who have it, and wrongly flags 10% of those who do not. Someone tests
positive. Roughly what is the chance they have it?
`About 8%` · **lure `About 90%`** · `About 50%` · `About 10%`
*Reading the test's accuracy as the answer and dropping the base rate.
True value 8.3%.*

**B2.** A conference has 80 nurses and 20 surgeons. You meet someone
decisive who enjoys pressure. Which is more likely?
`They are a nurse` · **lure `They are a surgeon`** · `Equally likely`
*A weak stereotype overriding a 4:1 base rate.*

**B3.** Which gives the better chance of winning: drawing one winning
ticket from a bowl of 10, or nine winning tickets from a bowl of 100?
`One from 10` · **lure `Nine from 100`** · `The same`
*The larger number of winners feels like better odds. 10% against 9%.*

## Family C: conjunction

A specific, plausible story cannot be more likely than the general case
that contains it.

**C1.** Dan reads two papers a day and argues about politics. Which is
more likely?
`Dan is a teacher` · **lure `Dan is a teacher who attends council
meetings`** · `Equally likely`
*The detailed version fits the description better and is a subset of the
first.*

## Family D: belief bias in syllogisms

A believable conclusion that does not follow. Different mechanism from
the others, which is why it is here: these load the logic rather than
the arithmetic, and they are short and robust.

**D1.** All roses are flowers. Some flowers fade quickly. Therefore some
roses fade quickly. Does the conclusion follow?
`No` · **lure `Yes`** · `Cannot tell`
*True-sounding and invalid. The flowers that fade need not be roses.*

**D2.** No experienced drivers are reckless. Some reckless people are
young. Therefore some young people are not experienced drivers. Does the
conclusion follow?
`Yes` · **lure `No`** · `Cannot tell`
*Valid but awkward, so it feels wrong. Included deliberately: without it
every "feels wrong" answer would be the correct one and the item set
would teach a strategy.*

---

## Notes for review

**D2 is doing structural work.** If every believable conclusion were
invalid, a participant could score well by answering "no" to every
syllogism, and by block 3 some will have noticed. Keeping the mapping
between how an answer feels and whether it is right genuinely mixed is
what stops the item set being gameable. The same applies across families
and is why the lure is not always the "obvious" option.

**B2 and C1 need the owner's eye most.** Both depend on a stereotype
being weak enough that the base rate should win, and that judgement is
cultural and can age badly. If either reads as relying on an assumption
about nurses or teachers that you would not want on the site, say so and
it gets rewritten rather than defended.

**A3 and A8 share structure with famous items** (lily pads, widgets) and
not surface. Judgement call: the structure is the thing being tested and
there are only so many structures. If you would rather avoid them
entirely, both can be replaced.

# Batch 2: the remaining sixteen

Counts, now complete: **A 10, B 8, C 4, D 8, total 30.** All arithmetic
machine-verified.

## Family A, continued

**A9.** A bag of flour weighs 3 kg plus half its own weight. What does
it weigh?
`6 kg` · **lure `4.5 kg`** · `3 kg` · `9 kg`
*Adding half of the stated 3 kg rather than solving W = 3 + W/2.*

**A10.** A clock takes 6 seconds to strike 4 o'clock. How long does it
take to strike 8 o'clock?
`14 seconds` · **lure `12 seconds`** · `16 seconds` · `8 seconds`
*Doubling the time with the hour. The gaps between strikes are what take
the time, and there are three at four o'clock, not four.*

## Family B, continued

**B4.** One bag holds 10 red marbles and 90 blue. Another holds 1 red
and 4 blue. Which gives the better chance of drawing red?
`The second bag` · **lure `The first bag`** · `The same`
*Ten reds feels like more chance than one. 20% against 10%.*

**B5.** A condition affects 1 person in 10,000. A test is right 99% of
the time. Someone tests positive. Roughly what chance do they have it?
`About 1%` · **lure `About 99%`** · `About 50%` · `About 90%`
*The same trap as B1 at a rarer base rate, where the gap is starker.
True value 0.98%.*

**B6.** A depot runs 85 white vans and 15 red. A witness says the van
was red, and is right 80% of the time. How likely is it the van was red?
`About 40%` · **lure `About 80%`** · `About 15%` · `About 60%`
*Taking the witness's reliability as the answer. True value 41%.*

**B7.** One polling station counts about 500 ballots a day, another
about 50. Which is more likely to report a day where over 60% went one
way?
`The smaller station` · **lure `The larger station`** · `Equally likely`
*Small samples swing further. Size feels like it should produce extremes
rather than damp them.*

**B8.** Of 1,000 people tested, 5 have a condition. The test flags all 5
of them, and also flags 95 who do not have it. Someone is flagged. What
is the chance they have it?
`5%` · **lure `100%`** · `50%` · `95%`
*"Flags all 5" reads as a perfect test. Deliberately the easier natural
frequency framing, as a contrast against B1 and B5: if these are
answered well and those badly, the framing is doing the work rather than
the arithmetic.*

## Family C, continued

**C2.** Maya studied biology and volunteers at an animal shelter. Which
is more likely?
`Maya works in finance` · **lure `Maya works in finance and fosters
animals`** · `Equally likely`
*The second fits the description and is contained by the first.*

**C3.** Which is more likely in the next five years?
`A major flood somewhere in Europe` · **lure `A major flood in Europe
caused by a dam failure`** · `Equally likely`
*A cause makes an event easier to picture and cannot make it more
likely.*

**C4.** Tom is 34, methodical, and keeps detailed records of everything.
Which is more likely?
`Tom plays an instrument` · **lure `Tom plays an instrument and
practises every day`** · `Equally likely`
*Daily practice fits methodical. It is still a subset.*

## Family D, continued

Eight syllogisms as a balanced 2x2, and the balance is the point.

|  | conclusion sounds true | conclusion sounds false |
|---|---|---|
| **valid** | D5, D6 congruent | **D2, D4 conflict** |
| **invalid** | **D1, D3 conflict** | D7, D8 congruent |

The two conflict cells are the measurement: belief pulling against
logic, in both directions. The two congruent cells are controls and they
are what stop the set being gameable. Without them a participant could
answer "it does not follow" whenever a conclusion sounded true and score
7 of 8. With them, neither trusting the feel nor inverting it beats
reading the argument.

**D3.** All nurses are trained in first aid. Some people trained in
first aid work in schools. Therefore some nurses work in schools. Does
it follow?
`No` · **lure `Yes`** · `Cannot tell`
*Believable and invalid. The school workers need not be the nurses.*

**D4.** All metals float. Gold is a metal. Therefore gold floats. Does
it follow?
`Yes` · **lure `No`** · `Cannot tell`
*Valid, with a false premise and an absurd conclusion. Validity is about
the argument, not the world, and this is the item most likely to be
argued with.*

**D5.** No birds are insects. All sparrows are birds. Therefore no
sparrows are insects. Does it follow?
**`Yes`** · `No` · `Cannot tell`
*Valid and believable. A control: feel and logic agree.*

**D6.** All doctors have a degree. Some doctors work nights. Therefore
some people who work nights have a degree. Does it follow?
**`Yes`** · `No` · `Cannot tell`
*Valid and believable, with enough clauses to need reading.*

**D7.** All vegetables are grown in soil. Some things grown in soil are
poisonous. Therefore all vegetables are poisonous. Does it follow?
**`No`** · `Yes` · `Cannot tell`
*Invalid and absurd. A control in the other direction.*

**D8.** Some teachers are novelists. All novelists are wealthy.
Therefore all teachers are wealthy. Does it follow?
**`No`** · `Yes` · `Cannot tell`
*Invalid: only "some teachers are wealthy" follows. The over-strong
conclusion is the tell.*

# Practice items

Three, shown before anything is scored. **They carry lures too.** A

test and would leave a participant's first real trap arriving at
question one, which is the worst place for it.

thirty structures twice so nothing is burned.

**P1.** A coffee and a muffin cost $7.50 together. The coffee costs
$6.50 more than the muffin. How much is the muffin?
`$0.50` · **lure `$1.00`** · `$0.75` · `$1.50`

**P2.** All cats are animals. Some animals are nocturnal. Therefore some
cats are nocturnal. Does it follow?
`No` · **lure `Yes`** · `Cannot tell`

# What I would want checked hardest

**D4 (gold floats) is the one most likely to be argued with.** It is
valid and its conclusion is false, which is exactly the point: validity
is a property of the argument, not of the world. Some participants will
answer "no" because gold does not float, and that is the measurement
working rather than a bad item. But if you would rather not defend it in
an email, it can be replaced.

**B8 is deliberately easier** than B1 and B5, in the natural-frequency
framing people handle better. That contrast is informative: if B8 is
answered well and B1 and B5 badly, the framing is doing the work rather
than the arithmetic, which is a finding in itself.

**Position of the correct option is not yet set.** Items are
standardized so position is fixed for everyone, which means it must be
deliberately varied across the set or a participant could learn a
position habit. That is a mechanical pass at authoring time rather than
a design question.

**Order across the thirty is not yet set**, and it matters more than it
looks: the block each item lands in determines the load it is answered
under, and families should be spread so no block is all arithmetic or
all syllogisms.
